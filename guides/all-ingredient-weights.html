How the numbers were worked out

Each density is the cup weight divided by 236.6 millilitres, so every row works out on its own: multiply the density by the cup volume and you get the cup weight back. The metric cup figure is the US cup weight times 250 divided by 236.6, and the tablespoon figure is the cup weight divided by 16. Where two sources disagree, the table uses the midpoint and says so in the source column, for flour, sugar, powdered sugar, brown sugar, and dry pasta. The kitchen converter on this site uses the same densities as this table, so its cup weights match the guide figures.

Which cup is which

A US customary cup holds 236.6 millilitres. A metric cup, used in Australia, New Zealand, and Canada, holds 250 millilitres. A UK cup is also 250 millilitres in modern recipes, so it takes the metric cup figure. When a recipe gives grams, use the grams and skip the cup entirely.

Frequently asked questions

Why does one cup weigh a different amount for each ingredient?

Because a cup measures volume and a gram measures weight. The link between them is density, grams per millilitre, and every ingredient has its own density. Water is 1.00 grams per millilitre, flour is about 0.52, and honey is 1.42, so the same cup holds very different weights.

Which figure should I use for flour, 120 g or 125 g?

Either, if you know where it came from. King Arthur lists 120 grams a cup and the USDA figure works out to 125 grams. This table uses 122.5 grams, the midpoint of the two, and the flour guide explains the range.

How accurate are these weights?

They are approximate. Density varies by brand, grind, moisture, and how the cup was filled, so two cups of the same ingredient can differ by a few grams. For baking, weigh the ingredient instead of trusting any cup figure.

Where do the densities come from?

Each density is the cup weight divided by 236.6 millilitres, the volume of a US cup, so every row works out. The cup weights come from the guides on this site, which cite USDA FoodData Central and King Arthur, except corn syrup, which uses the FSANZ specific gravity table, and vegetable oil, which uses the Codex Alimentarius range.

Every figure on this page is approximate, because density varies a little by brand and temperature, and by how the cup was filled. For exact work, weigh the ingredient.

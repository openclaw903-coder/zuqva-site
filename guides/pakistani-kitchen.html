Pakistani kitchen measurements: atta, ghee, and desi recipes in grams and cups

Desi cooking has its own measurement language. Recipes passed down in families talk about a katori, a chammach, or a handful, and even printed recipes often say one cup of atta without saying how that cup was filled. That works fine when the cook already knows the dough by feel, and it fails when a beginner tries to follow the same recipe with a different cup, a different brand of flour, or a scale instead of a bowl.

Atta is the place to start, because roti dough is the most forgiving teacher and the most common source of confusion. Whole-wheat atta is widely listed at about 120 grams per US cup, which gives a density of about 0.507 grams per millilitre. That means 500 grams of atta — a typical amount for a family batch of rotis — is about 987 millilitres, or about 4.2 US cups. If your recipe says four cups and your dough feels dry, the likeliest cause is not the recipe but the cup: a heaped, packed cup can hold far more than 120 grams, and atta absorbs water differently depending on grind and freshness. Add water a little at a time, and judge by the dough, not only by the number.

Suji, or semolina, is the next ingredient worth weighing rather than scooping. Coarse semolina is listed at about 167 grams per US cup, giving a density of about 0.706 grams per millilitre. A halwa recipe calling for one cup of suji is therefore asking for about 167 grams, and 200 grams of suji is about 283 millilitres, or 1.2 cups. Suji also swells as it cooks and absorbs ghee and sugar syrup, so the volume you start with tells you little about the volume you finish with. Weigh it, toast it evenly, and add syrup gradually.

Ghee deserves a warning of its own, and this site deliberately does not convert it by density. Ghee is solid when cool and liquid when warm, so the same weight takes up different volumes at different temperatures, and published cup weights vary accordingly. A recipe that says one cup of ghee usually means melted, measured ghee, but some cooks measure it solid and packed, and the two are not the same. The safe approach is to melt the ghee, measure it by volume if the recipe is written that way, or weigh it if the recipe gives grams, and never to convert between the two with a single factor you found online. If you see a converter that gives ghee a single density without mentioning temperature, treat that number with suspicion.

Besan is another ingredient to handle with care, and it is omitted from this site's converter for a stated reason: published cup weights conflict sharply, from about 85 grams per cup in one systematic chart to about 140 grams per cup in a dip-and-sweep chart. That gap is too large to paper over with one average. For pakora batter, the batter's consistency matters more than the starting weight anyway — it should coat the back of a spoon and fall in a slow ribbon. Use the recipe's weight if it gives one, adjust with water until the batter looks right, and do not trust a single converted number for besan until a better source settles the figure.

A few habits make desi recipes far more repeatable. First, pick one cup and use it for the whole recipe — do not mix a chai cup, a measuring cup and a katori. Second, spoon flour into the cup and level it, rather than scooping straight from the bag, which packs it down. Third, when a family recipe works, write down the weights the next time you make it. That one act turns a recipe you can only make yourself into a recipe anyone can make.

Desi cooking is not imprecise cooking. It is cooking where the cook's hands have done the measuring for years. A scale and a conversion table do not replace that experience, but they do let a beginner get close enough, fast enough, to start building it. Use the kitchen converter for atta and suji, read why grams and millilitres differ, and keep tasting and adjusting — the recipe is a guide, and your hands are the final judge.

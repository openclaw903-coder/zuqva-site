TDEE Calculator

Your TDEE — total daily energy expenditure — is an estimate of how many calories you burn in a day. It starts with your BMR, the energy your body uses at rest, then adjusts for how active you are.

Worked example

A 30-year-old man, 80 kg, 180 cm, moderately active: BMR = 10×80 + 6.25×180 − 5×30 + 5 = 1,780 kcal/day. TDEE = 1,780 × 1.55 = 2,759 kcal/day. Cut target = 2,259, maintain = 2,759, bulk = 3,259. Try those exact numbers in the calculator above — you should get the same answer.

What macros look like at 2,759 calories

Macros split that total across protein, carbs, and fat. The calculator does not work out macros, so here is the worked example by hand, using one common split: 30 percent protein, 40 percent carbs, and 30 percent fat. Not a prescription, and not medical advice.

Protein and carbs each carry 4 calories per gram, and fat carries 9. At 2,759 calories a day, that is about 828 calories of protein, 1,104 of carbs, and 828 of fat, or about 207 g of protein, 276 g of carbs, and 92 g of fat.

A second formula: Harris-Benedict

The calculator uses Mifflin-St Jeor. Harris-Benedict, published by Harris and Benedict in 1918, is an older formula many other calculators still offer. Men: BMR = 88.362 + 13.397 times weight in kg + 4.799 times height in cm minus 5.677 times age. Women: BMR = 447.593 + 9.247 times weight in kg + 3.098 times height in cm minus 4.330 times age.

For the worked example, Harris-Benedict gives 88.362 + 1,071.76 + 863.82 minus 170.31 = 1,854 calories a day, 74 above the Mifflin-St Jeor figure of 1,780, a difference of about 4 percent.

Katch-McArdle and body fat

Katch-McArdle starts from lean body mass instead of total weight, so it needs a body fat percentage. BMR = 370 + 21.6 times lean body mass in kg, where lean body mass is weight times 1 minus body fat as a decimal.

For the same 80 kg man at 20 percent body fat, lean body mass is 80 times 0.80 = 64 kg, and BMR is 370 + 21.6 times 64 = 370 + 1,382.4 = 1,752 calories a day, 28 below the Mifflin-St Jeor figure of 1,780. It needs a reliable body fat measurement, so this calculator does not ask for one.

Calorie safety floors

A cut target should not keep dropping. The minimums most often quoted are 1,200 calories a day for women and 1,500 a day for men. They are general guidance, not medical advice, and not a target to aim for. If TDEE minus 500 falls below the floor for your sex, use the floor instead and talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before going lower.

The formula, in full

Men: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5. Women: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161. This is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. TDEE = BMR × activity factor, using the factors shown in the form above.

How to read your result

The calculator gives you three numbers. BMR is what your body burns at rest: breathing, keeping your heart beating, staying warm. TDEE is BMR multiplied by your activity factor, so it is the number that matches a normal day, not a day spent lying still. That is why TDEE, not BMR, is the figure to plan your eating around.

Under your result you will see three targets. Maintain is your TDEE itself: eat about that many calories and your weight should stay roughly where it is. Cut is your TDEE minus 500 calories, and Bulk is your TDEE plus 500. Those 500-calorie steps are a starting point, not a rule, a smaller deficit or surplus works too, and it is often easier to stick to. The full reasoning is in how to use your TDEE for weight loss, maintenance, or gain.

How accurate is this estimate?

This is an estimate, not a measurement. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation uses only age, sex, height and weight, so it cannot see your body composition, and muscle burns more energy at rest than fat does, so two people with the same numbers on the form can have different real BMRs. For most people BMR is around two thirds of daily energy use, with the rest coming from movement, exercise and digestion, but the exact share depends on how active you are.

Treat the result as a starting point and check it against reality: if your weight is steady over two to three weeks while eating near your TDEE, the estimate is close. If it moves steadily, adjust your intake by a few hundred calories and watch again. And recalculate whenever your weight has changed by several kilograms and settled there, a smaller body has a smaller TDEE. The limits are explained in full in what TDEE is and how it is calculated.

Common questions

Should I eat my BMR or my TDEE? Your TDEE. BMR is what you would burn lying still all day; nobody eats for a day spent lying still. The difference between the two is explained in BMR vs TDEE.

Why does my TDEE jump when I change the activity level? Because the activity factor multiplies your BMR, moving from 1.2 (sedentary) to 1.55 (moderately active) raises the result by almost a third. Choosing honestly matters more than choosing hopefully; if you are unsure, see how to choose your activity level.

Do I need to recalculate often? Not daily. Recalculate when something real changes: your weight has shifted by several kilograms, your job or training routine has changed, or a few weeks at your target calories have not moved your weight the way you expected.

What macros should I eat at my TDEE? There is no single right split. One common split is 30 percent protein, 40 percent carbs, and 30 percent fat. At the maintain target of 2,759 calories a day, that is about 207 g of protein, 276 g of carbs, and 92 g of fat. The cut and bulk versions are in the macros table above.

Which BMR formula should I use? Mifflin-St Jeor is a good default, and it is the one this calculator uses. Harris-Benedict gives 1,854 instead of 1,780 for the worked example, a difference of 74 calories. Katch-McArdle is useful only if you know your body fat percentage.

What is the lowest calorie target I should use? As general guidance, many sources cite 1,200 calories a day as a floor for women and 1,500 a day for men. Do not go below those figures without professional guidance. If your TDEE minus 500 falls below your floor, use the floor as your target and talk to a doctor or registered dietitian.
